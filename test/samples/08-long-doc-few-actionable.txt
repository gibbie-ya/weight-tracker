The History and Principles of Ayurvedic Medicine — Reference Notes

Ayurveda is one of the world's oldest holistic healing systems, developed in India more than 3,000 years ago. The word Ayurveda derives from the Sanskrit words "ayur" (life) and "veda" (science or knowledge), meaning "knowledge of life." It is based on the belief that health and wellness depend on a delicate balance between mind, body, and spirit.

The Doshas
Central to Ayurvedic theory are the three doshas — Vata, Pitta, and Kapha — which are derived from the five elements (earth, water, fire, air, and space). Each person has a unique combination of the three doshas, with one or two typically dominant; this is known as the individual's prakriti, or natural constitution. Disease arises when the balance of doshas is disturbed (vikriti).

Vata governs movement and is associated with air and space. People with dominant Vata tend toward creativity and energy but may be prone to anxiety and digestive issues when out of balance.

Pitta governs metabolism and transformation and is associated with fire and water. Dominant Pitta individuals are often focused and driven, but imbalance may manifest as inflammation or irritability.

Kapha governs structure and lubrication and is associated with earth and water. Dominant Kapha people are calm and steady but may tend toward weight gain and lethargy when imbalanced.

Treatment Approaches
Ayurvedic treatment is highly individualised and may include dietary adjustments, herbal remedies, yoga, meditation, and Panchakarma (a five-step detoxification process). Practitioners take a detailed consultation covering physical constitution, lifestyle, digestion, sleep, emotional wellbeing, and medical history.

Modern Relevance
Interest in Ayurveda has grown significantly in the West over the past two decades. Some Ayurvedic herbs such as ashwagandha and turmeric have been subject to clinical research, with some positive findings around stress, inflammation, and cognitive function, though the evidence base is uneven and further rigorous trials are needed.
